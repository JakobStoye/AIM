## Fractional regime: exact conditioning and prediction

For H different from one half, the two-sided Wiener kernel is square integrable at its singularities and negative infinity. Translation, dilation and polarization give exactly the normalized target covariance; high Gaussian moments provide a continuous modification. The filtration is the driving Brownian past, including negative times, rather than an unjustified identification with the fBm natural filtration. For s>=0, the innovation integral over (s,r) is independent of that past, with variance q_H(r-s)^(2H). The conditional Gaussian convolution formula is therefore exact.

The conditional mean-increment estimate follows by differentiating the kernel on (-infinity,s] and integrating its squared derivative; its norm is proportional to (u-s)^(H-1). The noise mean has uniformly bounded variance, so the exponential derivative weights retain every finite moment. Comparability of delayed times justifies all instances of this estimate; the proof does not invoke it at a zero conditioning delay.

Lemma 3.1's Euler-state prediction is valid even when s is inside an Euler cell. The state at the next grid point is already measurable at s because that full step only uses its preceding grid noise and state. Freezing that state and the noise at s produces a past-measurable predictor. State freezing costs a squared interval length, and noise freezing costs an interval length to the power 1+H. Since H<1 and the interval is bounded, the former is absorbed by the latter. Conditional contraction yields

```math
\|Z^n_{\eta(t)}-\mathbb E^s Z^n_{\eta(t)}\|_p
\le C_p(t-s)^{1+H}.
```

The difference of two predictions follows by the tower property and conditional contraction. This is the extra state regularity needed for the sewing defect, and no independence of the predicted state from the conditional mean is assumed.

## Proposition 3.2: signed quadrature without exponent loss

The delayed conditional increment is defined using both the conditional expectation outside the integral and the predicted Euler state inside it. The direct noise-increment bound gives ell*h^H. If ell>=2h and the interval starts after its own length, the grid time remains at least ell/2 after the conditioning time. Heat spatial and variance derivatives respectively give h*ell^(H-1) and ell^(-H)*h*ell^(2H-1); integrating yields h*ell^H. The initial interval is treated separately near zero and then integrates h*r^(H-1), which is integrable for every H>0. The transition ell in [h,2h] and ell<h are correctly covered by the direct bound. Hence the claimed minimum of those two scale costs is valid.

For the conditional parent-minus-children defect, the tower property reduces the issue to the difference of the parent and child state predictions. At scales no larger than 4h, the deterministic state Lipschitz bound gives ell^(2+H), which is bounded by a constant times h*ell^(1+H). At larger scales both child conditioning delays are at least ell/4. The mixed heat bounds, conditional mean/variance increments, and prediction difference give h*ell^H per unit time, hence h*ell^(1+H) after integration. Conditional projection at the parent's earlier time does not increase the norm. On the interval beginning at zero, parent and children all condition at zero, so the defect is exactly zero.

At a fixed dyadic level, each parent defect is measurable at that interval's left endpoint: the right child's conditioning time is precisely that endpoint. Subtracting its conditional expectation one interval earlier therefore creates a genuine martingale-difference sequence. The L4 square-function inequality is proved directly: the cross sum has orthogonal L2 increments, conditional Jensen controls preceding partial sums by the final L4 norm, and the remaining sum of squared increments is bounded by the sum of their L4 norms squared. This does not discard correlations in the unconditioned noise.

Consequently each level costs the square root of its number of intervals times the minimum scale cost, plus the conditional mean cost. The conditional sums converge to the actual quadrature: predicted-state replacement costs ell^(1+H) after summing intervals, and the Gaussian innovation costs ell^H. The latter is at most the variance bound for a delay of 2ell; already-past grid noise has zero projection cost.

For ell>=h the martingale costs sum as h*sum ell^(H-1/2), and for ell<h they are bounded by h^(H+1/2). The first is dominated by its smallest scale when H<1/2 and its largest scale when H>1/2. The conditional mean costs are summable as h*sum ell^H. This proves the exact exponent H+1/2 or one, with no epsilon loss. The borderline logarithm at H=1/2 is explicitly excluded here and addressed separately.

## Brownian quadrature and bounded drift

Lemma 4.1 is sound for arbitrary globally Lipschitz functions. For a smooth mollification, the sawtooth time weight turns the cell quadrature into the semimartingale differential of f(W). Its average of forward and right-endpoint backward integrals has the right second-derivative signs. The deterministic bounded-variation weight has zero quadratic covariation. The intermediate-time correction is bounded by h times a Brownian increment, whose maximum over the block is bounded by twice the Brownian path maximum and therefore has Lp norm of order sqrt(d).

For reverse time Y_u=W_(d-u), the Gaussian bridge transition gives the stated reverse conditional mean. Adding the integral Y_u/(d-u) gives a martingale with quadratic variation u; Levy's characterization makes its martingale part Brownian in that reverse filtration. The finite-variation term has integrable endpoint norm because its integrand has size (d-u)^(-1/2) in every finite Lp. Reversing the right-endpoint integral gives the negative reverse Itô integral with the displayed orientation. Its martingale suffix maximum is controlled by twice the full martingale maximum, and its drift cost is bounded by h*Lip(f)*integral |W_s|/s ds, whose Lp norm is of order h*sqrt(d). The forward martingale has the same bound. Gaussian mollification converges uniformly with unchanged Lipschitz constant, completing the nonsmooth case independently of f(0) or an argument shift.

Lemma 4.2 uses Girsanov with a bounded progressive drift. The sign of its density is correct: under the changed measure W+integral u/beta is Brownian, so q is its affine Brownian image. Forward and inverse density moment bounds follow by factoring powers into exponential martingales and bounded quadratic-variation terms. Their cost is uniform when M^2*d is bounded, giving the claimed quadrature bound after Holder at a doubled moment exponent. At a finite-grid stopping time, future Brownian increments are independent of the past and the block length and clipping parameters are already measurable. Conditioning gives the same uniform estimate. This reasoning does not condition Brownian motion on a future localization event.

## Proposition 4.3: nonzero a(0), zero crossings and localization

The logarithm is only used on a sign interval where Z is nonzero. The random ODE makes log|Z| a finite-variation process there; the log drift is precisely alpha-beta^2/2+b_sigma(q)+sigma*a(0)*exp(-q). If a(0)=0, the transformed state either vanishes identically or stays a deterministic positive distance from zero in magnitude. Its logarithmic drift is bounded, and summation by parts with bounded state and state variation gives order h directly.

For a(0) nonzero, reflection of the state and drift allows a(0)>0. On E_K, J and its inverse are between 1/K and K. The state and its time derivative are bounded by constants times K. In the specified band of width proportional to 1/K, the positive constant-drift term dominates the a1 term; the derivative has a positive lower bound proportional to 1/K. Therefore Z can traverse the band only monotonically and cannot later re-enter it from above by a downward crossing. Each positive or negative expanded small annulus is traversed at most once, with total time bounded by C*K*epsilon. This justifies the counting argument even if the state starts at or crosses zero.

The inner cutoff delta is proportional to K*sqrt(h). Under h<=c_*K^(-4), its expanded band stays in that monotone region. Every outer block starts at a grid stopping time and has a known dyadic distance epsilon and length no greater than epsilon^2/(D*K^2). The sufficiently large fixed D ensures state variation at most epsilon/4 throughout the block, since epsilon<=C_Z*K on E_K. Thus the sign persists and |Z/J|>=epsilon/(2K). The clipped logarithmic drift agrees with the original on that event and is globally Lipschitz and bounded by C*K/epsilon. Its squared bound times the block length is uniformly bounded. Strong uniqueness of the clipped SDE consequently identifies its path with the actual logarithm throughout the block on E_K.

The conditional quadrature primitive has Lp supremum norm at most C*h*epsilon/K. The noise quadrature differs from the primitive weighted by Z at preceding grid points by a freezing error bounded by C*K*h per unit time: both state variation and the linear time correction are controlled, and the logarithmic ratio is used only with magnitude bounded away from zero on that block. Summation by parts costs C*epsilon because both magnitude and discrete variation are bounded on the block. A contribution therefore has Lp norm at most C*h*epsilon^2/K after restricting to E_K.

For epsilon<r0/4, the annular residence-time and nonfinal block-length bounds give at most C*K^3/epsilon blocks plus a final truncation. Summing the resulting h*K^2*epsilon costs over the dyadic small annuli is finite. For larger outer blocks, every nonfinal length is at least c*K^(-4), so there are at most C*K^4, each costing at most C*h*K. These estimates retain only a polynomial dependence on K.

The random enumeration paragraph is essential and correct. Successive starts in a fixed annulus are finite-grid stopping times. On E_K, only a deterministic bounded number can occur; retain just those first starts, with absent starts contributing zero. Each conditional error bound remains applicable before restricting to the future event. For the large annuli, the extra restriction epsilon<=C_Z*K is already known at the start. One may use unconditional norms of the clipped errors to bound their products with E_K and then Minkowski. No independence of E_K from the errors is needed, and a random number of unconditioned terms is not silently replaced by its expectation.

Every inner cell stays within twice delta on E_K and their total length is at most C*K*delta. The a1 noise coefficient is at most C*delta, so their entire quadrature is bounded by C*K^3*h times the largest Brownian within-cell increment. Its Lp norm is O(sqrt(h*log(2n))) and in particular uniformly bounded. This gives the required polynomial multiple of h. Larger steps are covered by the direct sqrt(h) bound and h>c_*K^(-4).

The final localized estimate has order h times K^6 at every needed finite moment. The Brownian supremum gives a tail decaying as exp(-c*(log K-C)^2) for the integrating-factor maximum. Applying Holder with the eighth moment on each dyadic shell makes the polynomial localization costs summable, uniformly in n. Thus the unrestricted L4 a1 quadrature has order h, including zero initial values and crossings. The constant-drift quadrature follows from the stated Itô differential of J; its drift coefficient is -alpha+beta^2, as required by the extra Itô correction.

## Final stability and degenerate cases

For the non-Brownian regime, state freezing gives Ch plus the deterministic Lipschitz error sum; freezing c costs at most h*TV(c) with bounded weighted moments. Proposition 3.2 supplies the remaining signed noise quadrature. For the Brownian regime, the exact-state residual is used instead of the Euler-state residual, permitting Proposition 4.3 and the constant-drift bound to apply. Both routes give the same discrete Gronwall inequality in L4 with the requested exponent.

The final factor J_T is exactly shared by the exact and numerical quotients. Holder at exponents four and four converts the transformed L4 estimate to the required terminal L2 estimate. If beta=0, deterministic Euler consistency and stability give order one; if a=0 the scheme is exactly homogeneous. Finitely many large step sizes can be included by the finite moment bounds and an enlarged fixed-data constant. No mesh-dependent localization constant remains in the final assertion.
