## AIM 142 — zero-frequency argument for a polygon

The published headline is for triangles. I checked the actual proof in Sections2–4, rather than extending that headline by analogy. At zero frequency its Fourier coefficient identity, spatial mollification, small-tip-annulus estimates and long orbit averages require only a compact oriented flat double with finitely many cone tips of positive finite angle. A simple polygon's double has exactly these properties, also at reflex vertices: embedded flat disks have radius at least a fixed constant times distance from the finite tip set, and each tip neighborhood has area O(radius²). Neither positivity of curvature nor a three-tip identity enters those estimates.

The two successive limits prove square-integrable horizontal derivatives for a bounded invariant function; the sharp zero-frequency energy inequality makes both vanish. The function is consequently independent of position on the connected polygon interior. Crossing any open side gives invariance under its direction reflection. Two adjacent sides at an irrational angle generate an irrational rotation, so every nonconstant angular Fourier coefficient vanishes. Applying this to bounded invariant-set indicators proves ergodicity of the full Liouville flow. This checks the submitted extension for the complete simple-polygon target; it does not attribute an all-polygon theorem statement to the triangle paper or require weak mixing at nonzero frequency.

## AIM 216 — explicit conductor counterexample

On the upper unit half-disk put C=P_radial+(1/16)P_tangential and u=r^(1/4)sin(theta)=y r^(−3/4). The matrix is measurable, symmetric and uniformly elliptic. Direct polar differentiation gives div(C grad u)=0 away from zero; its finite H1 energy and vanishing small-circle flux give the weak equation throughout the domain. Its boundary trace is g=y. On the flat positive diameter its outward conormal flux is −(1/16)x^(−3/4), which is not square integrable. Any candidate L2 flux must agree with that classical flux on each subinterval away from the origin, so the trace cannot belong to the flux domain.

The constant trace1 does belong to the domain, with zero harmonic flux. The globally smooth multiplier g=y thus violates the domain inclusion in the original target. The actual Meyers 1963 example on printed p.204 has exactly radial/tangential eigenvalues1 and mu² and this separated solution, with mu=1/4. The argument verifies the boundary application directly; it does not import an interior gradient failure as if it alone were a flux theorem.
