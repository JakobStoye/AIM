## Geometry and full target

The double of any bounded simple polygon is a compact connected oriented flat surface away from its finitely many vertices. Its vertex cone angles are twice the polygon interior angles, positive and finite. Reflex vertices give cone angles above a full turn; the proof never requires positive curvature or an upper cone-angle bound. In each fixed cone, scale a finite flat-disk cover of its compact unit-radius circle. This gives an embedded flat disk of radius at least a fixed positive multiple of distance to the tip. A cone tip neighborhood has area proportional to radius squared. Compactness away from the finitely many tips supplies the same two estimates globally. These are precisely the geometric inputs of source Section 3, equation (7), pp.6–7.

Almost every geodesic exists for all times and preserves Liouville measure. A finite-time incomplete path is Cauchy in the metric completion and can stop only at a tip. In a developed cone, a geodesic tending to the tip is eventually radial, so no infinite winding or unrecorded infinite side-crossing itinerary is introduced by a reflex angle. Vertex-hitting trajectories are a countable union of unfolded finite-itinerary position/direction conditions of measure zero. Folding the double gives the requested specular billiard factor, with the same normalized position-times-uniform-direction measure. These facts verify source Section 2, pp.3–4, for nonconvex simple polygons as well as triangles.

Take the indicator of any invariant billiard set and lift it to a bounded invariant function f on the double's unit tangent bundle. Its local equation is Xf=0. To prove ergodicity it suffices to show this indicator is constant. We need neither arbitrary unbounded eigenfunctions nor a product-flow spectral criterion.

## Reconstruction of the analytic argument at zero frequency

Oriented flat-chart changes have constant unitary rotation factors. Angular coefficients are consequently sections of flat unitary line bundles, and the forward and perpendicular derivatives have the stated two-mode Cauchy-derivative formulas. Integration by parts for a spatially compact smooth coefficient gives equal squared norms of its two complex derivatives. No boundary term at a cone tip is taken.

For a spatially compact field w, put Dj equal to the squared norm of the holomorphic first derivative of coefficient j. At zero frequency, the coefficient equations give a difference Dj−Dj+2 paired with (Xw)j+1. Sum over all consecutive indices from m to N. The high endpoints vanish by square summability of the smooth field's spatial derivatives; Cauchy–Schwarz bounds the remaining pairing by the product of the norms of Xw and of the gradient of w where Xw is supported. Thus Dm+Dm+1 has this same bound. This is the zero-frequency content of Lemma 3.1, pp.5–6; it needs only flat unitary transition functions, not a triangle identity.

The radial convolution in Lemma 3.2 is well defined in the embedded disks just proved, commutes locally with the two horizontal derivatives, and has the usual derivative scaling and bounded-input estimates. Apply it to a bounded local solution of Xh=0, then cut off across annuli of width proportional to epsilon around all tips. The annuli have area O(epsilon squared), while each error derivative is O(1/epsilon). The preceding coefficient estimate therefore bounds each coefficient gradient uniformly in epsilon and in its index. Weak convergence identifies the gradient of each original coefficient. This establishes Lemma 3.3, p.7, for every bounded local solution, not only globally invariant ones. That last distinction is essential for the second limit below.

Fix an angular cutoff m. The upper Fourier tail U has, distributionally, XU=B, where B has just the two lower endpoint modes. The two coefficients of B are respectively the antiholomorphic first derivative of fm and the negative first complex derivative of fm−1. The upper endpoint derivatives disappear weakly as their frequencies tend to infinity, using the uniform individual-coefficient bounds and angular Parseval. This verifies equation (13), p.8, without assuming the full gradient of f is already square integrable.

For fixed s>0 let T=s/epsilon and retain only states whose entire orbit segment of length T stays farther than a constant times epsilon from every tip. A time mesh with spacing epsilon has O(T/epsilon) points. Measure preservation and the tip-area estimate bound the discarded measure by O(s+epsilon squared). Average the truncated bounded f backward along these orbit segments for time T. The average g is still bounded, differs from f in norm by O(square root of s+epsilon squared), vanishes near every tip, and satisfies a derivative bound of order 1/T. This derivative is an exact difference of the two time endpoints divided by T; no derivative of the discontinuous good-set indicator is taken.

Now spatially smooth g. Its zero extension has a whole collar of vanishing values, so it introduces no boundary distribution. The smoothed field w has Xw tending strongly to zero as epsilon tends to zero at fixed s, while YXw is bounded by a constant times 1/s. Testing against compact regular functions and using density gives YXw converging weakly to zero. The distributional tail equation can therefore be tested against Yw: finite angular sums approximate that test and its horizontal derivatives because w has square-integrable spatial derivatives of every order. Commutation of X and Y gives the desired endpoint pairing tending to zero. This checks equations (15)–(21), pp.8–10, including the potentially delicate test admissibility.

At fixed s the coefficient-gradient bounds for w may deteriorate like 1/s; they are nevertheless finite and permit identification of the relevant four coefficient-gradient weak limits in a bounded local solution hs. This yields the endpoint pairing with hs. Since Xhs=0 locally, Lemma 3.3 applies again and restores coefficient bounds independent of s. Moreover hs tends strongly to f as s tends to zero. Passing through this second limit gives the exact endpoint pairing with f. There is no interchange of limits, no claim that YXw tends to zero in norm, and no assumption that hs is globally flow invariant. These are the steps in equations (22)–(24), p.10.

At zero frequency the two selected modes of Yf are exactly −2i times the antiholomorphic first derivative of fm and +2i times the holomorphic first derivative of fm−1. Pairing them with B gives twice i times the sum of their squared norms. The exact zero pairing forces both derivatives to vanish, for every m. Hence all coefficient gradients vanish, and angular approximation shows the spatial gradient of f vanishes distributionally. This conclusion can be obtained directly at zero frequency without auditing a nonzero-frequency energy estimate or spectral weak-mixing theorem.

## Reflection step and conclusion

The polygon interior is connected, including when it is nonconvex. Restricting to its first sheet with the ordinary Euclidean direction coordinate, the vanishing spatial gradient makes f a function c of direction alone. An open-side crossing chart is a single connected regular flat chart on the double. Its two expressions therefore identify c(v) with c(Rv), where R is reflection in that side direction. This uses equality on open sets and does not require a boundary trace of the original measurable indicator.

Choose an irrational interior angle. The two adjacent side reflections compose to rotation by twice that angle, also when the vertex is reflex. Its ratio to a full turn is irrational. Each nonzero angular Fourier coefficient of c is therefore multiplied by a scalar different from one under an invariance that leaves it unchanged, so it vanishes. Thus c, f and the original invariant-set indicator are constant almost everywhere. The indicator is zero or one, proving ergodicity of every polygon allowed by the target and ruling out the requested example.
