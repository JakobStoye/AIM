Combining these two gains and subtracting a small component's ball leaves a positive separation term when its diameter is sufficiently small. The medium-component estimate must handle a subset with many vertices. The compact grid union does this: its deterministic empty shell is valid outside the entire union, and the cone collar surplus follows from the full Brunn--Minkowski inequality, including a zero-volume set. For each anchor cube the shape count is finite independently of n. The strict positive volume surplus beats all face-critical powers. This excludes all medium separated subsets, not just components at one radius.

At least one critical face attains the minimum log-log coefficient. A thin normal patch on that face has diverging expected isolated-vertex count at the lower radius. The second-moment close-pair surplus and disjoint-ball logarithm expansion show relative variance tends to zero. This proves the random last-isolation radius lies above the deterministic lower radius; a mere leading strong law would not suffice here.

For large components I reconstructed the connected separator argument, including the zero-diameter case and the use of the two-component small-ball-complement property. The published Lemma 3.14 and Proposition 3.15 provide precisely unicoherence and that complement property for convex polytopes. A single deterministic net covers every possible connected separator at every larger graph radius. Its near-separator small balls contain no sample point, by the exact distance s/2 identity; their disjoint positive-volume sections allow an exponentially small void bound. Choosing one fixed large diameter threshold excludes two large components simultaneously for all s above the lower radius, without a continuum union bound.

At any such radius a nonsingleton component can be neither small nor medium, and at most one can be large. Thus a disconnected graph must have an isolated vertex. At the actual closed radius L_n none does, so it is connected and the exact equality M_n=L_n follows. This checks the random-radius lift rather than substituting convergence of the ratio.
